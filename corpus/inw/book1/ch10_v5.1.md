# Chapter 10: Push and Pull

## The Rubber Band

Neil handed Sophie a thick rubber band.

"Stretch it," he said.

Sophie hooked the band around her fingers and pulled. It stretched, resisting her with increasing force.

"Feel that?" Neil asked.

"It's pulling back," Sophie said. "The more I stretch it, the harder it pulls."

"Why do you think it pulls back?"

Sophie thought. "It... wants to return to its normal size?"

"Good. But *why* does it want to return?"

Sophie looked at the stretched band. She thought about atoms vibrating, oscillating in balanced beats.

"The atoms in the rubber," she said slowly. "They're normally vibrating at a certain natural spacing. When I stretch the band, I'm forcing them farther apart than they want to be. They're trying to return to their natural vibration?"

Neil smiled. "Exactly! When you stretch the rubber band, you're **constraining** the oscillations. The atoms can't vibrate freely at their natural spacing—they're being held in a stretched configuration. That constraint creates what we call **tension**—a pulling force trying to return the system to equilibrium."

Sophie released the band. It snapped back to its original shape.

"The atoms returned to their natural spacing," she said.

"Yes. When you removed the constraint, the stress was released, and the system returned to balance."

"There's a name for that snap-back ability," Neil added. "**Elasticity**. It's the measure of how readily something stores the stress you put into it and then springs back to balance when you let go. A rubber band has lots of it; a lump of clay has almost none—push the clay and it just stays pushed."

"So elasticity is how *willing* a thing is to return to balance," Sophie said.

"Beautifully put. And here's the part to tuck away: Nature has a single, fixed measure of elasticity built into its very fabric—a constant that says exactly how much any balanced oscillation resists being constrained and how hard it pushes back. It isn't a property of rubber, or steel, or any one material. It belongs to the substratum itself—the underlying fabric that fills all of space—and it holds the same everywhere, in every direction, at every scale. Every rubber band, every spring, every atom borrows its springiness from that one universal constant. We'll give it a proper name and a number in a later book. For now, just hold the idea: elasticity is a fundamental property of the substratum, everywhere the same, and it's constant."

"Can I measure it?" Sophie asked.

"Measure what?"

"The stiffness. You said elasticity is a number. So different rubber bands should have different numbers."

Neil grinned. "Exactly what I was hoping you'd say." He handed her a paper cup, a bag of small coins, and a ruler. "Tie the rubber band to the shelf hook so it hangs straight down. Hook the cup to the bottom. Measure how far down it hangs — that's your zero. Then add coins one at a time and measure the new length each time."

Sophie set it up. She marked the zero point on a strip of paper taped to the wall, then dropped in the first coin.

The band stretched a little. She marked the new position.

Second coin. It stretched a bit more. Third, fourth, fifth. Each time she marked the position on the paper strip.

When she had eight marks she stepped back and looked.

"They're evenly spaced," she said. "Each coin adds about the same amount of stretch. One coin, one tick. Two coins, two ticks. It's proportional."

"Write those numbers down," Neil said. "Coins on one side, stretch on the other."

She did:

| 2     | 8            |
| 3     | 11           |

| 5     | 19           |

"Not exactly even," she said, frowning at the 11 where 12 should be. "But close. About 4 millimeters per coin."

"Plot it," Neil said, handing her the graph paper.

Sophie drew the axes — coins across, stretch up — and dotted in the eight points. They sat very nearly on a straight line, rising steadily from the bottom left.

"A line," she said. "The slope is the stiffness. The steeper the line, the more it stretches per coin — the softer the band."

"Now try this." Neil handed her a second rubber band, identical to the first, and showed her how to loop it alongside the original so both bands carried the cup together, side by side.

Sophie repeated the experiment. This time each coin moved the cup only about 2 millimeters. The line was shallower.

"Two bands sharing the load," she said. "Each one only has to take half the pull, so the cup drops half as far. Same coins, less stretch. The pair is stiffer than one band alone."

"That number — how much force per unit of stretch — is called the **stiffness constant**," Neil said. "Every elastic thing has one: one band, two bands together, guitar string, rubber ball, steel beam. It's a fixed ratio for that object, the same way your pendulum had a fixed constant for its place."

Sophie looked at her two lines, side by side on the graph paper. Two bands, two slopes, two numbers.

"So stiffness isn't a feeling," she said. "It's a measurement. A ratio. A constant."

"And once you have it," Neil said, "you can predict how much a load will stretch or compress that material — but only within a certain range. As long as you stay inside it, the line stays straight: double the load, double the stretch. Push past it and the material stops being linear — the line bends, and eventually it gives way. Engineers design based on that number, and they respect its limits."

## Compression and Tension

Neil produced a metal spring—a stiff coiled wire spring.

"Now compress this," he said, handing it to her.

Sophie pushed the ends of the spring together, squeezing the coils.

"I feel it pushing back," she said. "Like it wants to expand."

"Same principle," Neil said. "The atoms in the metal are being pushed closer together than their natural spacing. They're constrained in a compressed configuration. That creates a pushing force—**compression stress**—trying to return the system to its natural state."

Sophie released the spring. It expanded back to its normal length.

"So stretching creates tension—pulling stress. Compressing creates compression—pushing stress. Both are the same thing: constrained oscillations—the natural back-and-forth vibrations of the very atoms that make up the band or the spring—wanting to return to balance."

"Exactly," Neil said. "Whether you pull atoms apart or push them together, you're constraining their natural vibration. That constraint creates stress, and stress creates force."

He held up a finger. "But here's something that's easy to miss. Tension and compression aren't really *one* force each—every one of them is *two* opposing forces locked together. When you stretch the band, your pull is met by the band's pull-back: two pulls in opposite directions. When you squeeze the spring, your push is met by the spring's push-back: two pushes in opposite directions. There's always a pair, always opposing each other."

Neil pressed his own two palms together in front of his chest, fingers pointing up—the Namaste greeting.

"Remember what you told me about this gesture?" he asked. "Your two hands as a conjugate pair—mirror partners meeting in the middle." Sophie nodded; they'd talked about it after her standing-wave discovery. "Good. Now let's use it to feel something new."

"Here's the simplest way to feel stress with your own body," he said. "Press your palms together. Push hard—but don't let either hand move."

Sophie pressed her palms together and pushed.

"Feel that?" Neil asked. "Your hands aren't going anywhere. Nothing is moving. But there's a real force right there between your palms—you can feel it. That's *stress*: a force squeezing inward in compression, with no movement at all."

Sophie pushed harder, feeling the pressure build between her unmoving hands.

"That's compression stress," she said. "Force without going anywhere."

"Now the opposite," Neil said. "Hook the fingertips of your two hands together, like two teams in a tug-of-war, and pull them apart—but don't let either hand move."

Sophie curled her fingers together and pulled outward. Her hands stayed locked in place, but she could feel the strain running across her knuckles.

"My hands still aren't going anywhere," she said, "but now I feel it pulling instead of pushing."

"That's tension stress," Neil said. "The same still, motionless force—but pulling outward instead of squeezing inward. Compression and tension, the push and the pull, both real, both with nothing moving at all."

"Right. The word 'stress' just means that pressing-force—the push or the pull inside the material. One thing worth keeping clear: this is *physics* stress, the kind an engineer measures in a bridge or a beam. 'Stress' here means a force pressing or pulling—it's purely a measure of force inside a material."

## Interactive Stress

They sat at a picnic table in the park.

"There's a term for this," Neil said. "When balanced beats are constrained—when they can't oscillate freely—the system experiences **interactive stress**."

He wrote in her journal:

**INTERACTIVE STRESS: Constrained oscillation creating push-and-pull in a system**

"It's called *interactive*," Neil said, "because it's never just sitting there—it's *dynamic*. Push and pull, compression and tension, squeezing in and stretching out, working against each other at the same time. When you stretched the rubber band, your pull and the band's pull-back were interacting. When you pressed your palms together, each hand pushed against the other. Two forces meeting and pressing—that interaction *is* the stress."

"So interactive stress is the push and the pull happening together," Sophie said.

"Exactly. Compression on one side, tension on the other, in constant dynamic interplay. That's interactive stress."

"And there's a reason we say *interactive* and not just 'a force,'" Neil went on. "A single force, all on its own, would just send something flying off in a straight line. But here the forces aren't independent—they're penned in. The constraint holds them together so they have to act on *each other*, as a set, over and over: push provokes push-back, which provokes push again. It's a *cycle* in a closed-in space, not a one-and-done shove. That give-and-take, looping inside the constraint, is exactly what 'interactive' means."

Sophie nodded slowly. "So 'interactive' means the forces are a team that keeps answering each other—because they're trapped together and can't just escape."

"That's it precisely. And there's a way to *feel* that back-and-forth in your own body," Neil said. "Hold the rubber band stretched out as far as you can, and keep holding. Or press hard against the spring and don't let up. After a while, what happens?"

Sophie thought about it. "My hands start to shake. They get all quivery."

"Right—your muscles begin to **quiver**. That quiver is the whole idea in miniature. When you can no longer simply *pull* the band apart or *push* the spring together with one steady effort, your muscles start rapidly switching—pull, give, pull, give—shuttling back and forth between the two. That tiny, fast reversal *is* the constrained system trying to hold balance. The shuttling reversal between pulling and pushing is a phase all its own, and it has a name we'll meet soon. For now, just feel it: under enough stress, a steady force turns into a rapid back-and-forth."

Sophie flexed her trembling hand. "So the quiver is the reversal—the system shuttling between push and pull because it can't hold either one forever."

"Exactly. Hold on to that feeling. It's one of the most important ideas in the whole book."

He paused. "And here's something important. The ancient Sankhya texts have a Sanskrit word for exactly this: **Duhkha**."

Sophie leaned in, curious.

"In the authentic ancient Sanskrit, Duhkha means *interactive self-similar stress*," Neil said. "That's physics stress—the very thing we're talking about: systems under constraint, oscillations that can't move freely, forces created by interaction. And *self-similar and scale invariant* because the same kind of stress shows up at every scale, from a stretched rubber band to a vibrating atom to a whole galaxy."

"So it's a physics word," Sophie said.

"Exactly. This rubber band carries Duhkha when you stretch it. The spring carries Duhkha when you compress it. It's the stress of interaction, repeating the same pattern at every size."

Sophie stared at the rubber band in her hand with new understanding.

"So when we constrain Nature's balanced beats," she said, "we create Duhkha—interactive self-similar stress in the system."

"Yes. And that stress is what creates forces—push and pull."

## The Spring That Trades Motion

The next morning Sophie found Neil setting up something on the doorframe.

A heavy steel spring hung from a hook at the top. At the bottom of the spring, a solid brass cylinder sat clamped tight, its flat ends sticking out on either side.

"Pull it down about five centimeters and let go," he said.

Sophie pulled the brass weight down and released it. The spring bounced — up, down, up, down — a clean, even bounce.

Then, after maybe fifteen bounces, something strange happened.

The bouncing got smaller. Not because it was running down — it was too soon for that — but because the motion was *going somewhere else*. The brass cylinder began to twist. Slowly at first, then faster, winding one way, unwinding, winding the other. A rotation where there had been none.

"I didn't touch it," Sophie said. "Where did the twist come from?"

"Keep watching."

The twist grew. The bounce shrank to almost nothing. For a few seconds the cylinder just sat there, quietly spinning back and forth while the spring barely moved up and down at all.

And then the trade reversed.

The twist died away and the bounce returned — smooth, steady, as if it had never left. Up, down, up, down. The whole cycle started again.

"It keeps doing that," Neil said. "Bounce trades into twist, twist trades back into bounce. Over and over, through the same spring, with nothing added or removed."

Sophie crouched down and watched another full cycle. Bounce → twist → bounce. She could follow the energy crossing from one kind of motion to the other, as clearly as she had followed it crossing the rubber band between the two coupled pendulums in Day 6.

"It's the coupled pendulums," she said. "But different. The pendulums traded motion between two separate objects through a rubber band. This trades motion between two kinds of motion — bouncing and twisting — through one single spring."

"Same exchange," Neil said. "Different geometry. The spring can stretch along its length *and* twist around its length. Those two freedoms are coupled together inside the coils. When the bounce stores energy in the spring by compressing it, some of that stored stress can leak into torsion — the spring twists a little. That torsion grows at the bounce's expense until all the energy has migrated into twisting. Then the process reverses."

"So the bounce and the twist are like the left washer and the right washer," Sophie said, "and the spring itself is the rubber band — the coupling."

"Exactly. This setup is called a **Wilberforce pendulum**, after a physicist who studied it in the 1890s. It's the clearest case of two kinds of motion exchanging energy through a single elastic element. No motor, no battery, no outside push. Just one spring, one weight, and the interactive stress inside doing all the work."

Sophie watched another cycle, timing the full trade and return: about twelve seconds.

"Is this what you meant by interactive stress being *dynamic*?" she said. "Not just sitting there balanced, but actively trading back and forth?"

"That's exactly it. The stress isn't static. It's an exchange — energy shuttling between two stores, compression and torsion, through the constraint of the spring. The same exchange you felt in your quivering arm, the same exchange the coupled washers showed you, but here in its cleanest form: one object, one spring, two kinds of motion, a perfect trade."

Sophie straightened up and looked at the spring, still patiently cycling.

"If you can't build this at home," Neil added, "try the crude version. Take a heavy washer and hang it from a long, thick rubber band that you twist tightly before letting go. The washer bounces and twists at the same time, and if you watch carefully you can see the energy favoring one, then the other. It's rougher than the Wilberforce, but the trade is unmistakable."

## Structures Under Stress

They walked to a nearby pedestrian bridge—an arched structure spanning a small creek.

"This bridge is constantly under interactive stress," Neil said.

"How?" Sophie asked.

"The arch is under **compression**. Weight pushing down on it gets distributed through the curve, squeezing the materials together. Feel the stone here—millions of atoms pushed slightly closer than their natural spacing, creating compression stress."

He pointed to the cables supporting the handrail.

"These cables are under **tension**. They're being pulled, stretching very slightly—atoms pulled slightly farther apart than their natural spacing."

"But the bridge isn't moving," Sophie said.

"Because it's in equilibrium. All the forces balance. The compression in the arch balances the weight of the structure and the people crossing. The tension in the cables balances the pull from the handrails. Interactive stress exists throughout, but it's balanced—which is why the bridge is stable."

Sophie ran her hand along the arch, trying to feel the invisible compression.

"So materials can be under stress but still stable," she said.

"Yes. In fact, most structures *need* to be under stress to work properly. The stress is what holds everything together. As long as it doesn't exceed the material's strength, and as long as forces stay balanced, the structure stands."

"And if stress exceeds strength?"

"The material breaks. The constraint fails. We'll explore that more later, but for now, just know: interactive stress is natural and necessary. It's not a bad thing—it's how structures work, how materials hold their shape, how forces act."

## Push and Pull Through Empty Space

On the way home they stopped at Neil's workbench, where two round magnets sat on the table.

Neil slid one magnet toward the other. While they were still a finger's width apart, the second magnet skittered away on its own.

"It moved before they touched," Sophie said. "Nothing pushed it."

"Something did," Neil said. "You just couldn't see it. Bring the same poles together and they push apart; flip one around and they pull together—and the push or the pull reaches across the gap between them. No string, no rod, no contact at all."

Sophie held the two magnets close and felt the invisible cushion springing between them, pushing back against her hands.

"It's the same push and pull as the rubber band," she said slowly, "but with nothing in between."

"That's the puzzle that troubled people for centuries," Neil said. "How can one thing push or pull another across a gap with nothing to carry the force? Einstein had a nickname for that kind of reaching across a distance—he called it 'spooky action at a distance.' It seemed like magic: a force with no visible messenger."

"So how does it work?"

Neil smiled. "That's a question for a later book. But here's a clue to carry with you. It only looks spooky if you believe the space between the magnets is truly empty. What if it isn't? What if space is filled—everywhere—with the same elastic substratum whose constant we talked about this morning? Then the magnet isn't reaching across nothing at all. It's pushing on what fills space, and that push travels through, exactly the way tension travels down a stretched rubber band."

Sophie looked at the gap between the magnets with new eyes—not as emptiness, but as something.

"So it isn't spooky," she said. "We just haven't met the thing in the middle yet."

"Exactly. Hold on to that. Once you know what fills space, action at a distance stops being magic and becomes just one more push and pull in Nature's Wheelwork."

## Everything Pushes and Pulls

As the day progressed, Sophie started seeing forces everywhere.

**Gravity:** Constant downward pull on everything.

**Friction:** Resistance when surfaces slide past each other.

**Air Pressure:** Atmosphere pushing on everything from all sides.

**Muscle Force:** Her muscles pulling on bones to create movement.

**Magnetic Force:** A magnet's pull or push reaching across a gap—even through what looks like empty space.

All of them were pushes or pulls. All of them involved interactive stress—constrained oscillations creating forces.

"Even gravity?" she asked Neil.

"Even gravity," he confirmed. "We'll explore the details much later, but yes—gravity is also constrained oscillation in the universal substratum. When mass is present, it constrains the oscillations of space itself, creating the 'pull' we call gravity. It's all interactive stress at different scales and configurations."

Sophie's mind reeled. Every force—from the pull of her muscles to the pull of planets on each other—was constrained balanced beats creating stress.

Push and pull. Tension and compression. Balance and imbalance. All expressions of interactive stress in Nature's Wheelwork.

## Explore It Yourself

*Try these at home or at school. Some use stretchy objects; others use the free tools on the INW book series website (Imagine Nature's Wheelwork).*

#### Activity 1: Feel the Interactive Stress

**What You Need:** A rubber band or a spring

**Instructions:**
1. Stretch the rubber band slowly and feel it pull *back*, trying to return to balance.
2. Let go and watch it snap home.
3. Now squeeze a spring and feel it push the other way.

**Key Insight:** That pull (or push) is **interactive self-similar stress** — a force toward balance (the authentic ancient meaning of *Duhkha*). It is also a taste of **elasticity**: how readily a thing stores stress and springs back.

#### Activity 2: Push-and-Pull Tug

**What You Need:** Two people; a towel or rope

**Instructions:**
1. Each person grips an end and pulls gently against the other.
2. Hold steady so that nobody moves.
3. Pay attention to what your arms feel during the standoff.

**Questions:**
- Nobody is moving — so why can you both still feel a force?
- Where else might this standoff of balanced forces hide, even inside still, solid things?

#### Activity 3: Magnet Balance

**What You Need:** Two magnets

**Instructions:**
1. Push like poles together and feel the invisible "no thanks!" pushing back.
2. Flip one magnet and feel the pull instead.
3. Try to hold them at the exact spot where push and pull seem to tie.

**Key Insight:** That balance point — where opposing forces meet and hold — is exactly what atoms are always seeking.

#### Activity 4: Measure Stiffness

**What You Need:** A rubber band; if you can get them, an extension spring (the kind that resists being pulled longer) and a compression spring (the kind that resists being squeezed shorter); a paper cup; small coins or washers (all the same weight); a ruler; a strip of paper taped to the wall; graph paper

**Instructions:**
1. Tie the rubber band to a hook so it hangs vertically. Hang the cup from the bottom.
2. Mark the cup's resting position on the paper strip — that is your zero.
3. Add coins one at a time. After each coin, mark the new position.
4. When you have six to eight marks, measure the gaps in millimeters.
5. Plot coins (horizontal) against stretch (vertical) on graph paper.
6. Now try the two kinds of spring. Springs come in two flavors: an **extension spring** stores stress when you *pull it longer* — hang it like the rubber band and add coins. A **compression spring** stores stress when you *push it shorter* — stand it upright and press known weights on top, measuring how much it shortens. Plot each one the same way.

**Record:** The points should sit close to a straight line. Its slope — how far it moves per coin — is that object's stiffness constant. Add a second rubber band alongside the first and plot both: two bands sharing the load give a shallower slope, because the pair is stiffer than one. Then compare the extension spring's line (stretching) with the compression spring's line (squeezing).

**Key Insight:** Stiffness is a ratio, not a feeling. Extension and compression are the same story told two ways — pulling stress and pushing stress — and each spring has its own fixed constant you can use to predict how far it will move under any load, as long as you stay within its straight-line range.

#### Activity 5: The Twist-and-Bounce Trade

**What You Need:** A heavy washer or nut; a long, thick rubber band

**Instructions:**
1. Tie the washer to the bottom of the rubber band and hang the band from a doorknob or hook.
2. Twist the band about twenty turns, keeping the washer still.
3. Pull the washer down slightly and let go of everything at once.
4. Watch: the washer bounces *and* spins. See if the bounce sometimes dies away while the spin picks up, then the spin fades and the bounce returns.

**Record:** Note whether you can spot the trade — bounce shrinking while twist grows, then reversing. It may be rough and fast, but the exchange is there.

**Key Insight:** One elastic element (the rubber band) couples two kinds of motion (bouncing and twisting). Energy shuttles between them through interactive stress inside the band — the same exchange the coupled pendulums showed, but in a single object.
