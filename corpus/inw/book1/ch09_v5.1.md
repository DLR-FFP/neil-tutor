# Chapter 9: When Things Line Up

## The Chord and the Arpeggio

Sophie followed Neil into the school's music room. A piano sat in the corner, sunlight streaming across its keys.

"Sit," Neil said, gesturing to the bench.

Sophie sat, and Neil stood beside the piano.

"I'm going to play three notes," he said. "Listen carefully."

He pressed three keys at exactly the same time—C, E, and G.

A rich, full chord filled the room. The three notes blended into one sound—harmonious, complete, satisfying.

"Now," Neil said, "the same three notes, but differently."

He played C, then E, then G—one after another in quick succession.

The notes flowed together in a rolling pattern, an arpeggio that rippled through the air. The same notes, but the experience was completely different—not full and rich, but flowing and sequential.

"What changed?" Neil asked.

"The timing," Sophie said immediately. "The first time, all three notes happened together. The second time, they happened one after another."

"Exactly," Neil said. "Same notes. But when they happened *together*, you got one rich chord—intense, full, concentrated. When they happened *one by one*, you got a flowing arpeggio—extended, distributed, spread out."

He sat on the bench next to her.

"Today we're going to explore what happens when things line up versus when they happen in sequence. It's one of the most important principles in nature."

## The School Assembly

They walked to the school auditorium, where an assembly was about to begin. Students filled the seats—maybe a hundred people.

"Perfect timing," Neil said. He spoke briefly to the teacher in charge, who nodded and smiled.

The teacher stood at the front and called for attention.

"We're going to try an experiment," she announced. "We need everyone to participate. When I say 'GO,' everyone clap your hands once. Just one clap, but we all do it at exactly the same time. Ready?"

The students leaned forward in their seats.

"Three... two... one... GO!"

*CLAP!*

The sound was enormous. A single, sharp, incredibly LOUD clap that echoed off the walls.

Sophie's eyes widened. One hundred people clapping at the exact same moment sounded like a thundercrack.

"Now," the teacher said, "we're going to do a wave. Starting from this side of the room, each person claps one after the other, as fast as you can, like a ripple. Ready? Start!"

The first student clapped. Then the next. Then the next.

*Clap-clap-clap-clap-clap-clap-clap...*

The sound rolled across the auditorium like a wave, each individual clap distinct but flowing into the next. It took several seconds to reach the other side of the room.

When it finished, Sophie sat in stunned silence.

"Same number of people," Neil said quietly beside her. "Same number of claps. But completely different, right?"

"Completely," Sophie whispered.

## The Difference

Outside in the courtyard afterward, Sophie couldn't stop thinking about it.

"The simultaneous clap was so loud," she said. "But each person only clapped once. Why was it louder than any single clap?"

"Because they all happened at the same moment," Neil said. "When a hundred sounds occur at exactly the same time, they add together. Not a hundred separate claps—one clap with the intensity of a hundred."

"And the wave clap was quiet," Sophie said, "but it lasted so long."

"Right. When the claps happened one after another, the sound spread out over time—the same hundred claps, but instead of intensity, you got duration. Together, they create intensity. Spread out, they create duration."

"So timing changes everything," she said.

"Timing changes everything," Neil confirmed.

## The Array Comes Back Together

Sophie suddenly stopped walking.

"The pendulum array," she said. "From the beach. That's what happened there, isn't it?"

Neil waited.

"We released all twelve washers at the same instant — simultaneous. They moved as one wall. Then they spread apart, each one keeping its own time, and it looked like chaos. But then they gathered themselves back and swung together again."

"And why did they come back together?" Neil asked.

"Because of how we cut the strings." Sophie pulled out her notebook and flipped to the page. "The lengths were chosen so the shortest pendulum did exactly 51 round trips in one minute and the longest did 62. Every one in between was evenly spaced. So after one minute they'd all completed whole numbers of swings and landed back in line at the same instant."

"Which means the re-forming wasn't luck," Neil said.

"It was arithmetic." Sophie tapped the page. "The lengths set the timing, and the timing guaranteed they'd line up again. Simultaneous → scattered → simultaneous, on a schedule."

"And between the two simultaneous moments?"

"Sequential. Each washer drifting through its own position, one by one, no pattern you could name. Until the numbers bring them together again." She looked up. "It's the chord and the arpeggio. The wall of washers is the chord. The chaos in between is the arpeggio. And the timing decides which you get."

## Beating

"Now simplify it," Neil said. "Forget twelve pendulums. Just two."

He found a length of string and cut two pieces — one at 50 centimeters, one at 52.

"Almost the same length," Sophie said. "They'll swing almost the same speed."

"Hang them side by side. Start them together and watch."

Sophie hung both from the bench armrest, pulled them back with a flat hand, and let go.

For the first few swings they moved in perfect step — both forward, both back, exactly together.

Then, slowly, the shorter one crept ahead. After ten swings the gap was visible. After twenty the two were completely out of step — one forward while the other was back.

And then, just as slowly, the shorter one lapped the longer. They drifted back into step. Together again.

"That's a **beat**," Neil said. "Two nearly equal rhythms, drifting in and out of step. Together — apart — together. You can *count* the drift."

"How long is one beat cycle?" Sophie asked, already reaching for the stopwatch.

She timed it. From one moment of perfect togetherness to the next: a little over a minute — about 70 seconds.

"The beat frequency is the *difference* between the two swing frequencies," Neil said. "Watch the photogates. In about a minute the 50 cm pendulum logs a gate count near 70 — roughly 35 round trips — and the 52 cm one about 66, or 33 round trips. Timed precisely, the first swings at 0.705 cycles per second and the second at 0.691. The difference is only 0.014 hertz — so one full beat cycle, together to apart to together, takes 1 ÷ 0.014, about 70 seconds."

Sophie wrote that down. "So the beat is slow because the two rhythms are *close*."

"The closer they are, the slower the beat. Two exactly equal pendulums would never drift apart — no beat at all. Two very different ones would beat so fast you couldn't follow it. The sweet spot is *nearly equal*: slow enough to watch, clear enough to count."

"And this is what the coupled pendulums did on Day 6," Sophie said. "The rubber band forced them to trade energy back and forth — that trade had a beat too."

"Same idea, different mechanism. On Day 6 the coupling was the rubber band — the trade was forced. Here there's no coupling at all. Each pendulum swings on its own. The beat is just what you *see* when you watch two free rhythms side by side. It's an observation, not a force."

Sophie watched the two washers drift apart and drift back, apart and back, in their slow, patient cycle.

"Everything lines up," she said, "given enough time."

"If the ratio is right," Neil said. "That's tomorrow's question."

## A New Vocabulary

Neil introduced the formal terms.

"When things happen at exactly the same time," he said, "that's **simultaneous activity**—everything together, in the same instant. We can call it *simultaneity* for short."

Sophie wrote the words in her journal: **SIMULTANEOUS ACTIVITY**

"When things happen one after another," Neil continued, "that's **sequential activity**—one thing, then the next, then the next, in order. *Sequence* for short."

She wrote: **SEQUENTIAL ACTIVITY**

"Simultaneous activity creates **intensity**—things concentrated, amplified, strong," Neil said.

**INTENSITY**

"Sequential activity creates **duration**—things spread out, distributed, lengthened."

**DURATION**

Sophie looked at the four words.

"So the simultaneous clap had high intensity but no duration. The wave clap had high duration but low intensity."

"Exactly," Neil said. "Same total energy—a hundred claps worth—but organized completely differently."

"And this works for more than just clapping?" Sophie asked.

"It works for everything," Neil said. "Think about music. When several notes sound together, you hear a *chord*—that's harmony, the simultaneous side: many tones stacked into one rich moment. When the same notes are played one after another, you hear a *melody*—that's the sequential side, the tones spread out in time so the tune unfolds step by step."

"So harmony is the clap-all-at-once and melody is the wave," Sophie said. "Same notes, but one piles them up and the other lays them out."

"Exactly. The simultaneous and the sequential are just two ways of arranging the very same thing."

## Waves Revisited

"Remember standing waves from Day 6?" Neil asked.

Sophie nodded.

"At the antinodes—the points of maximum vibration—two traveling waves arrive simultaneously and in phase. Their peaks line up perfectly in time. That simultaneous arrival creates intensity—the antinode vibrates with maximum amplitude."

"And at the nodes?" Sophie asked.

"At the nodes, two waves still arrive simultaneously, but they're out of phase. One wave's peak arrives at the exact same moment as the other wave's trough. They're opposite, so they cancel instead of amplify."

Sophie thought about this.

"So it's not just about things happening at the same time," she said. "It's about how they line up when they happen together."

"Yes! That's called **phase alignment**. When oscillations are 'in phase'—peaks line up with peaks—they reinforce each other. When they're 'out of phase'—peaks line up with troughs—they cancel."

"And if they don't line up neatly?" Sophie asked. "Not perfectly in phase, not perfectly out of phase—just somewhere in between?"

"Great question—and here's something that's commonly misunderstood," Neil said. "When two waves don't line up neatly, it's *not* that nothing happens, or that there's no interference. They still add together. But instead of a simple amplify-or-cancel, they create a brand-new, more complicated waveform—a *higher-order* wave with its own peaks and quiet spots in new places."

"So the nodes and antinodes just... move?" Sophie asked.

"Exactly. New pattern, new positions for the still points and the loud points. The waves are always interacting; they just build a richer shape when they're partly aligned. There's no such thing as 'no interference'—only different patterns of interference."

Sophie saw it now. Simultaneous arrival with perfect phase alignment created the dramatic effects—biggest amplification or full cancellation. Anything in between created a more intricate waveform, with the nodes and antinodes shifted to new places.

## Ratio Figures

After lunch Neil set up something new. He drove a hook into the underside of a shelf and hung a single string from it — long, about a meter. At the bottom he tied a paper cup with a small hole punched in it and filled with fine sand.

"Push it," he said, "so it swings in a straight line, like a normal pendulum."

Sophie pushed. The cup swung back and forth, leaving a thin line of sand on the dark paper Neil had spread below.

"Now give it a sideways nudge — gently — while it's already swinging."

She tapped the cup sideways. Immediately the line opened into an ellipse, the sand tracing a smooth oval.

"Two swings at right angles," Neil said. "Right angles just means the two directions cross square to each other, like the corner of this page — the back-and-forth going one way, the side-to-side going straight across it. Both happening at the same time. The shape you see is both of them *together*."

Sophie watched the sand trace the oval, round and round.

"Now. I'm going to change the setup." He replaced the single string with a Y-shaped cradle: one string from the hook splitting into two strings partway down, meeting again at the cup. By adjusting where the Y split, he could give the back-and-forth swing a different period from the side-to-side swing.

"This time the two swings have different speeds," he said. "The back-and-forth is twice as fast as the side-to-side. A 2-to-1 ratio. Push it and watch."

Sophie pushed. The sand didn't trace an oval. It traced a figure eight — a smooth, closed loop that crossed itself in the center, over and over.

"It's a figure eight!" she said.

"A 2:1 **ratio figure**," Neil said. "Two beats in one direction for every one beat in the other. The shape closes because the ratio is exact. Scientists sometimes call these *Lissajous figures*, after a Frenchman who studied them in the 1850s, but the idea is simpler than the name: two swings at right angles, and the ratio decides the shape."

He adjusted the Y-cradle.

"Now 3 to 2," he said. "Three beats one way for every two the other."

The sand traced a new shape — more intricate, with three lobes, but still a clean closed curve that retraced itself.

"Try 5 to 3," Sophie said.

Neil adjusted again. The cup swung, and the sand drew an even more elaborate figure — five crossings one way, three the other — but still closed, still stable, the same line retracing itself on every pass.

Sophie stared at the three sand patterns laid out on the dark paper.

"Now try one that isn't a clean ratio," she said.

Neil adjusted the Y-cradle to something between 3:2 and 5:3.

The cup swung. The sand traced a complicated tangle of curves that never quite retraced themselves. Each pass laid down a slightly different line, and after a minute the paper was covered in a fuzzy mess.

"It never closes," Sophie said.

"Because the ratio isn't exact," Neil said. "A clean whole-number ratio means the two swings line up again after a fixed number of passes — the figure closes and repeats. An in-between ratio means they *never* line up exactly, and the figure keeps drifting, never settling into a stable shape."

Sophie looked at the closed figures and the open mess side by side.

"So the shape is a *picture* of whether the two rhythms are in ratio — side-to-side compared to front-to-back, the x travel distance to the y travel distance," she said. "Closed means yes. Open means no."

"Exactly. A ratio figure is a visible record of timing. And the simpler the ratio, the simpler the figure: 2:1 gives a figure eight, 1:1 gives an ellipse, 3:2 gives three lobes. The more complicated the ratio, the more crossings — but as long as it's whole numbers, it closes."

## Light and Lasers

"Let's talk about light," Neil said.

He pulled out a small laser pointer and clicked it on. A bright red dot appeared on the wall.

"Regular light—from a light bulb or the sun—is made of countless photons (tiny packets of light waves) all vibrating at slightly different frequencies and different phases. The oscillations are random, out of sync. That's called **incoherent** light."

He clicked off the laser and pulled out a small flashlight.

"This flashlight produces incoherent light. Lots of photons, but they're not synchronized. Their oscillations happen at different times, randomly distributed. The light spreads out, diffuses, and isn't particularly intense."

He turned on the laser again.

"But a laser produces **coherent** light. All the photons oscillate at the same frequency *and* in phase. Their waves line up simultaneously, creating incredible intensity—all the light energy concentrated into a narrow, powerful beam."

Sophie looked at the bright laser dot on the wall.

"Same light—electromagnetic waves," Neil said. "But organized differently. Simultaneous and in-phase makes it intense and concentrated. Random timing makes it diffuse and spread out."

"Simultaneous versus sequential," Sophie said.

"Exactly."

## Everywhere You Look

As the day went on, Neil pointed out examples everywhere.

**Synchronized Swimmers:**
"They all dive at the same moment—their simultaneous timing creates visual impact. If they dove one by one, you'd get a sequence, extended over time, less dramatic."

**Orchestra Playing:**
"A chord is simultaneous—all instruments playing their notes at the same instant, creating rich harmony. A melody is sequential—notes one after another, creating flow and progression."

**Fireworks:**
"Finale with all fireworks bursting simultaneously—intense, spectacular. Fireworks launching one by one—extended, drawn out."

**Marching Band:**
"In step—everyone's left foot hits the ground simultaneously, creating a single loud thump. Out of step—feet hit at different times, creating a muddled sound distributed over time."

**Heartbeat:**
"All your heart muscle cells contract simultaneously—that's what creates the strong pulse that pumps blood. If they contracted in random sequence, it would be weak and ineffective."

Sophie saw the pattern everywhere now. Nature used both strategies—the simultaneous mode when intensity was needed, the sequential mode when duration or distribution was useful.

## The Deep Connection

As the sun began to set, they sat on a bench in the park.

"This simultaneous-and-sequential idea," Sophie said slowly, "it feels really important. Like more important than just clapping or music."

Neil nodded. "It is. It's one of the most fundamental principles in nature."

Sophie was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached for her notebook and flipped back through the pages, past the ratio figures, past the beating pendulums, all the way to the first day.

"Neil." Her voice was careful, as if she might spook the idea. "Look at the ratios I just drew. 2 to 1. 3 to 2. 5 to 3."

"I see them."

"Two, three, five." She underlined each one. "Those are *my numbers*. The bean numbers. The sequence from the flowers."

Neil said nothing. He just waited.

"I found them in petals on Day 1. I found them hiding in the counting triangle on Day 2. They showed up in the sunflower spirals on Day 4. And now they're here — in the shapes two pendulums draw when they swing at right angles." She looked up. "Same numbers. Four completely different doors."

"Five," Neil said, "if you count the poetry. Pingala was counting rhythms — and rhythms are two beats at a ratio, just like your sand-cup."

"Five doors," Sophie whispered. "Poetry, flowers, a triangle, spirals, and swinging washers. All arriving at the same set of numbers."

"And every one of those doors was open before anyone had a modern instrument," Neil said. One sentence, dropped without emphasis: "The rule behind these numbers was written down before anyone had a pendulum."

Sophie stared at the notebook. Five paths, five subjects, five centuries — and one set of numbers sitting at the end of each.

He paused, choosing his words carefully.

"Remember I told you that atoms are like standing waves? Oscillations creating stable patterns?"

Sophie nodded.

"Those oscillations are happening simultaneously," Neil said. "Trillions and trillions of tiny oscillations all vibrating together in the same tiny region of space. That simultaneous activity — that concentration of oscillations in one place — is what creates mass. What makes matter feel solid and heavy."

Sophie's eyes widened.

"Wait. Mass is... simultaneity?"

"In a way, yes," Neil said. "We'll explore this much more deeply later. But the basic idea is: when oscillations happen all together, in the same instant and in step, they're counted together, and that creates what we experience as concentrated matter — mass, substance, solidity. Scientists have a name for this kind of densely-packed, coherent matter: *hadronic* matter. Hold on to that word; you'll meet it again in the next books."

"And space?" Sophie asked. "Is space the sequential, spread-out part?"

"That's the tempting guess," Neil said gently. "You might think space is just 'stuff spread out' — expansion. But it isn't. *Space itself is perfectly still and perfectly coherent* — the calm, in-step background everything else dances on. Space is on the simultaneous, coherent side, not the spread-out side."

Sophie frowned. "Then what *is* the spread-out, one-after-another part?"

"Motion is," Neil said. "Expansion comes from *movement*, not from empty space. And oscillations move in two ways. One stays put and spins — a beat whirling around its own center. That's the part of matter we call *charge* — the *leptonic* part. The other travels from place to place, carrying energy. That's *radiation* — light and sound."

"So matter has two parts?" Sophie asked. "The all-together, coherent part — and a spinning part?"

"Almost. Real matter is both at once: a coherent, simultaneous *hadronic* part that gives it mass, and a spinning part we call charge. Both regions can spin — even the hadronic core carries charge. The real difference is that the *leptonic* region can compress and expand, while the hadronic region cannot; it stays incompressible. And separate from matter, there's radiation — light and sound — the *traveling* kind, moving as wavefronts that squeeze together and spread apart."

Neil brought his palms gently together. "Think about how your own body is built — still and coherent at its core, moving and spinning at its edges, both at once. That's not just matter. That's you, too." Sophie looked down at her steady hands, and for a moment she saw exactly what he meant.

"And every one of these modes breathes in two phases," Neil added, "a *compression* phase where things squeeze closer, and an *expansion* phase where they spread back out. Sound does it with air, light with the field, matter deep inside. Push and pull, over and over."

Sophie felt like her mind was expanding.

"So it's not 'matter together, space apart,'" she said slowly. "Space is the still, coherent background, a true expanse. Matter is coherent beats packed together — plus a spinning part. And light and sound are beats that travel, squeezing and spreading as they go."

"That's a beautiful way to put it," Neil said. "We'll refine all of it in the future books. But you've just grasped one of the deepest secrets of reality: timing matters. *When* things happen — together or one after another, still or moving — is as important as *what* happens."

## Explore It Yourself

*Try these at home or at school. Some use simple props; others use the free tools on the INW book series website (Imagine Nature's Wheelwork).*

#### Activity 1: Together or One-by-One?

**What You Need:** A few friends, or coins

**Instructions:**
1. Have everyone clap at the exact same instant — that is **simultaneous activity** (all together).
2. Now clap one after another around the circle — that is **sequential activity** (one-by-one).
3. Switch back and forth between the two.

**Questions:**
- The claps are the same — so why do the two arrangements feel so different?
- Which one feels like music's harmony, and which like its melody?

**Take it further online:** In the **Pendulum Lab Collection**, open the **Pendulum Wave** and switch between releasing all the pendulums at once (simultaneous) and letting them scatter into a one-after-another ripple (sequential) — the same claps-versus-wave idea, drawn in motion.

#### Activity 2: Two Pendulums Beating

**What You Need:** String; two similar weights (washers or nuts); a horizontal bar (broom handle across two chairs); a stopwatch

**Instructions:**
1. Cut one string at 50 cm and another at 52 cm. Hang both from the bar, close together.
2. Pull both back with a flat hand and release at the same instant.
3. Watch: they start together, drift apart, then come back together. Time how long one full "together → apart → together" cycle takes.
4. Now try 50 cm and 55 cm. Is the beat faster or slower?

**Record:** Write down both beat times. The closer the two lengths, the _______ the beat.

**Key Insight:** A beat is what you *see* when two free rhythms drift in and out of step — pure arithmetic, no forcing required.

**Take it further online:** Compare two setups in the **Pendulum Lab Collection**. The **Coupled Pendulum** links two pendulums with a spring so they *trade* energy back and forth — a forced beat. The **Pendulum Wave** lets a whole row drift in and out of step on their own. Watch how "coupled" and "free" produce the same in-and-out rhythm by different means.

#### Activity 3: Sand-Cup Ratio Figures (Tier 2)

**What You Need:** A paper cup; fine sand or salt; string; a hook in a doorframe or shelf; dark paper or a tray; tape

**Instructions:**
1. Punch a small hole in the bottom of the cup. Thread a long string (~1 m) through the rim and tie it to the hook so the cup hangs freely.
2. Spread dark paper underneath. Fill the cup with sand.
3. Set the cup swinging in a straight line. You should see a thin sand line on the paper.
4. While it is still swinging, tap it sideways. The line should open into an ellipse or a more complex figure.
5. For a cleaner ratio, use a Y-cradle: split the string into two branches partway down, adjust the split point to change the ratio. A 2:1 split gives a figure eight; 3:2 gives three lobes.

**Record:** Sketch each figure and write the ratio beside it. Try at least one ratio that is *not* a clean whole number — what happens?

**Key Insight:** A closed figure means the two rhythms line up. An open mess means they never do. The figure is a *picture* of whether the ratio is exact.

**Take it further online:** In the **Pendulum Lab Collection**, open the **Blackburn Pendulum** — a sand-cup swinging at right angles, exactly like this build. Set the two directions to whole-number ratios (2:1, 3:2, 5:3) and watch the closed figures trace themselves; nudge the ratio off a whole number and watch the figure refuse to close.

#### Activity 4: Line Up the Waves

**What You Need:** The **Waves Lab Collection** on the INW website (Imagine Nature's Wheelwork)

**Instructions:**
1. Open the **Standing Waves** sim and send two waves toward each other along the same line.
2. Slide them until their peaks arrive together (in phase). Watch the antinodes swell to maximum — simultaneous arrival, amplified.
3. Now shift one wave half a step so a peak meets a trough (out of phase). Watch the nodes fall still — simultaneous arrival, canceled.

**Questions:**
- What is the same in both cases, and what is different? (Hint: the waves *arrive together* either way.)
- Where have you seen "in phase" and "out of phase" before in this book?

**Key Insight:** Lining up in *time* is not enough — it is *how* two beats line up (peak-to-peak or peak-to-trough) that decides whether they add or cancel. That is phase alignment.
