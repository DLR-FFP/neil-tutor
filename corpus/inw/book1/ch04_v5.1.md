# Chapter 4: Spirals That Appear From Repeating Cycles

## The Question

![The face of a sunflower, its seeds packed in interlocking spiral arms. A few arms curving one way and a few curving the other are traced in gold, showing the two families of spirals — the parastichies — that this whole chapter is about.](illustrations/ch04_sunflower_parastichy.png)

*book1_ch04_01: The sunflower that starts it all. Two families of spiral arms — one winding clockwise, one counter-clockwise — traced over the seed head. Counting them, and asking why the counts are what they are, is the journey of this chapter.*

Sophie couldn't stop thinking about the sunflower.

It had been three days since she first counted the spirals at the Saturday market near the park—21 one way, 34 the other. Since then, she'd learned about the Fibonacci sequence, the golden ratio (φ ≈ 1.618), and the golden spiral. In Chapter 3 she'd measured shells and even grown her own 3D whorl in the Phi Growth Lab—watching a shape keep the *same* proportion as it swelled, turn after turn. That was growth caught and held still: one shell you could pick up, one shape frozen at one moment, phi governing how a thing grows *in space*.

But a sunflower head wasn't a frozen shell. It was the *record of an action repeated*—the same little turn taken over and over about a center, laying down one seed each time. Not a shape held still, but a rhythm you were seeing all at once. Chapter 3 had been about growth frozen in time. This felt like something else: a turning that *kept going*.

But one question kept nagging at her.

The seeds didn't just spiral outward. They were also *spaced*. Each seed had its own position, its own spot on the flower head. And somehow, the way they were placed didn't leave any blank space. No wasteful gaps, no bald patches—the whole face of the flower was filled edge to edge, every seed tucked into the room left by the ones before it.

How?

She pulled out the sunflower photo on her phone—the same one she'd taken that first day—and zoomed in.

The seeds formed these beautiful interlocking spirals, yes. But a whole cluster of questions bubbled up in her mind:

*What actually makes a spiral? What causes it? How many spirals are there — and why? Why does one set wind clockwise and another wind counter-clockwise? And why should the number of spirals going around the full circle be Fibonacci numbers, of all things?*

She thought about it. A spiral wasn't a thing you draw all at once. It was something that *grew*—just like the Fibonacci pattern Neil had shown her. New seeds were born at the center, one after another, and each one ended up a little farther out and a little larger, and turned a little around from the one before.

So a spiral was really two kinds of growth happening together: growing *outward* from the center (that's the **radius**, the distance part) and turning *around* the center (that's the **angle**, the rotation part). Radial unfolding and angular unfolding, step by step, seed by seed.

Chapter 3 had chased the outward part—the whorl that grows by phi and keeps its shape. This chapter, she sensed, was about the *other* half: the turning. The same step taken again and again about a center—a repeating, cyclic action, one seed dropped each lap.

It wasn't that the distance mattered more than the angle, or the angle more than the distance. It was the *growth pattern itself*—little packets, one seed at a time, each adding on and spreading out in a natural rhythm tied to phi.

"It's growth," Sophie muttered to herself. "A spiral is just growing outward and turning at the same time." She stared at the seeds, letting the questions pile up instead of rushing to answer them. Two families of arms, crossing. Counts that came out Fibonacci. No blank space anywhere. "There's *something* deciding where each seed lands," she said slowly. "I don't know what yet. But it's the same something, every single time."

She texted Neil: *Found something. Can we meet?*

His reply came thirty seconds later: *Sunflower farm, 2pm. Bring protractor.*

## The Farm

The sunflower farm was a sea of gold.

Rows and rows of sunflowers stretched toward the horizon, their massive heads turned slightly, tracking the sun. Sophie and Neil stood at the edge of the field, and the farmer—a woman named Betsy who knew Neil from some workshop or another—had waved them in and left a folding table and a ladder beside the nearest row.

Sophie was already eyeing the closest flower head. "Look at all the questions it's hiding," she said. "The seeds spiral outward, sure — but they're also spaced around the center, and there's no blank space anywhere. Two sets of arms, crossing. And when I counted them, the numbers came out Fibonacci." She tilted her head. "Something has to be deciding where each seed lands — the same something, every time, or it wouldn't come out this neat. Can we get at whatever it is?"

"We can," Neil said. "But not there, and not first." He laid a hand on the thick green stalk instead of the flower. "That head has a couple of *thousand* seeds packed edge to edge. Before we climb a ladder and stare into that, let's find the very same angle somewhere we can actually put a protractor on it."

Sophie raised an eyebrow. "Somewhere easier than the flower?"

"Start small," Neil said. "Then work up to the hard one. Look down the stalk—not the flower, the *leaves*."

Sophie crouched. The big leaves came off the stem one at a time, winding up it like a spiral staircase. Near the bottom they were broad and a little ragged; near the top they got smaller and newer, right up to the tight furl just under the flower.

"The bottom leaf is the oldest—it came out first," Neil said. "Every leaf above it is younger than the one below. You can *see* the order they were born, running straight up the stem, oldest to youngest. Now sight straight down the stalk from above and tell me how far around each leaf sits from the one just below it."

Sophie closed one eye and looked down the axis of the stem, so the leaves flattened into a wheel. She lined up the protractor as best she could.

"The next leaf up is turned about... 140 degrees. Maybe 135."

"And the one above that?"

"About the same. Every leaf is turned roughly the same amount from the one under it."

"That 'roughly the same amount,'" Neil said, "is about 137 and a half degrees. On a stalk with only a handful of leaves you can measure it with your own hands, because you can see which leaf came first and count up from there."

"137.5," Sophie said. "Is that related to the golden ratio?"

"It's called the golden angle, and it comes straight from the golden ratio. We'll see exactly how, later. For now—hold onto that number."

*book1_ch04_02 — Looking straight down a leafy stalk so the leaves flatten into a wheel; a protractor reads about 137.5° between one leaf and the next.*

Neil reached into his bag and set two things on the folding table: a pineapple and a pine cone.

"The leaf method doesn't scale," he said. "Once a plant packs dozens of parts close together, you can't slip a protractor between them anymore. So we stop measuring the angle and start *counting* what it leaves behind." He stood the pineapple upright on the table, stem-end down. "Here's the thing to get right, because most people get it backward. A spiral family isn't one row you chase from bottom to top. It's how many separate arms of the *same slant* you'd cross if you walked one full lap around the fruit. That count — the number of parallel arms going all the way around — is the number that matters."

Sophie leaned in. Each scale was a little diamond — really a four-sided trapezoid — and the diamonds lined up edge to edge in slanting lanes. "So I follow the edges of the diamonds," she said. "They point two ways."

"Exactly. The two slanting edges of each scale hand you the two main families. Pick the lanes leaning one way and count how many go around; then the lanes leaning the other way."

Sophie set a fingertip on one diamond and let her eye run along the lane of edges climbing gently to the right, marking her starting scale with a dab of Betsy's chalk so she'd know when she'd gone all the way around. "Eight lanes leaning right." She followed the steeper edges the other way. "And thirteen leaning left."

"Eight and thirteen," Neil said. "Seen those two together before?"

"Fibonacci," Sophie said. "They're next to each other in the sequence."

"There's a third family hiding too," Neil said. "Don't look at the edges this time — look straight across each diamond, corner to corner, the nearly flat direction. Follow those diagonals around." Sophie traced one across the vertices of the scales and counted them around the fruit. "Five." She sat back. "Five, eight, and thirteen — three families, every count a Fibonacci number. And each one's just a different way of connecting the same scales."

Neil handed her the pine cone. "Same idea, a little harder to see. Stand it upright and look at the *bottom* end, where the scales fan out from the base — that's where the two families read most clearly."

Sophie turned the cone base-up and let her eye follow the fanning scales one way, then the other, counting how many arms swept all the way around each time. "Eight one way..." She rotated it. "...and thirteen the other."

"A pineapple and a pine cone, and neither ever met a sunflower," Neil said. "Same two numbers, next-door in Fibonacci — and you found them with your own finger. No protractor, no ladder."

*book1_ch04_03 — A pineapple and a pine cone, each with two spiral families traced and counted — 8 and 13.*

Sophie looked up at the nearest flower. "Okay. *Now* can we do the big one?"

Neil steadied the ladder while she climbed and photographed the head from straight above. Back at the table, they spread the photos out.

"Same plan," Neil said. "Find the real turning step. On the leaves you *measured* it; on the pineapple you *counted* it. Try it here."

Sophie traced one of the bold spiral arms with her finger. "I follow this arm and measure from one seed to the next along it—"

"Try it, but watch out for one thing," Neil said. "The seed right next to it along that arm is *not* the one that was born next. The arms your eye follows are made of seeds born many steps apart. Trace an arm and you'll get the wrong angle every time."

"Then I'll do what I did on the stalk—number the seeds by birth order, oldest in the center, outward to youngest, and measure between the ones born in a row."

"Go ahead."

Sophie put her pencil to the tiny seeds crowded at the very center—and stopped. She couldn't tell which speck was seed one and which was seed two. They were packed too tight, too many, too nearly the same size to rank by eye. On the stalk she'd had six leaves in a clear line. Here she had two thousand seeds in a jam.

"I can't," she said. "On the leaves I could see the order. Here I can't even find seed one. There are too many, too close together."

"Now you've hit the real wall," Neil said—and he sounded pleased, not disappointed. "That's the honest end of what hands and a protractor can do. You measured the turn where the parts were few and spread out. You counted its fingerprint where the parts were many but still countable. Here the parts are so many and so tight that neither method reaches."

"So we're stuck?"

"On the flower, by hand—yes. To watch a head like this build seed by seed, we'll need a different kind of workspace: one that grows the pattern for you, one seed at a time, and can freeze it so you can look." He pulled out his phone. "Scientists have always done their experiments on a *lab bench*—a real table you set your apparatus on and try things. Ours lives on a screen instead of in a room, so we call them *digital benches*: same idea, same hands-on tinkering, just built out of light instead of wood. There's one in the INW Discovery Zone that does exactly this. But you already know what you'd be looking *for*."

"137 and a half," Sophie said. "The number from the stalk."

"137 and a half," Neil agreed. "Let's go back to the workshop and see whether that one angle can build a whole head from scratch—on paper first, where we're in charge of every step."

## The Test

Back at Neil's workshop, Sophie sat at the now-familiar wooden table with a blank sheet of paper, a compass, a ruler, and a protractor.

"I want you to try an experiment," Neil said. "Draw a circle. Then place dots around it, each one turned the same amount from the last. We're not going to decide ahead of time what *should* happen. We'll try different turns and just watch what each one does."

"Watch for what?" Sophie asked.

"For whatever pattern shows up," Neil said. "Try not to ask yet whether it's good or bad, or why a plant might want it. It's tempting to pick a reason first and then look for it — but that's backward. Let's just see what each turn *makes*, and let the pattern speak first. The right questions come after you've watched, not before."

Sophie drew her first circle.

"Start with 90 degrees," Neil suggested.

Sophie marked her first dot at the top of the circle — 12 o'clock. Then she used the protractor to rotate 90 degrees and marked the second dot — 3 o'clock. Third dot at 6 o'clock. Fourth dot at 9 o'clock.

And then, when she rotated another 90 degrees...

"I'm back at 12 o'clock," she said. "The fifth dot lands on top of the first dot."

"Right," Neil said. "90 degrees divides the circle into exactly four equal parts. After four rotations, you're back where you started. The pattern repeats."

"So 90 degrees doesn't work," Sophie concluded.

"Try 120 degrees," Neil suggested.

Sophie drew a new circle and started again. This time, after three dots, she was back at the starting position.

"Same problem," she said. "It repeats after three."

"Try 180 degrees."

That one repeated after just two dots.

Sophie sat back, frustrated.

"Every angle I try repeats! How do you get an angle that *doesn't* repeat?"

Neil's eyes twinkled.

"What kind of numbers repeat when you divide a circle?" he asked.

Sophie thought about it.

"Fractions," she said slowly. "Like 90 degrees is one-quarter of 360. And 120 is one-third. They're fractions of the circle."

"Exactly. Any angle that's a *rational* fraction of 360 degrees will eventually repeat. So what kind of number do you need?"

Sophie's eyes widened.

"An *irrational* number. Like phi."

"Exactly," Neil said. "And the golden angle — 137.5 degrees — is irrational. It's related to phi. So it never repeats. Ever."

Sophie was still looking at her three ruined circles.

"Paper and a protractor," she said. "Is that how you'd actually do it?"

"No," Neil said. "A protractor makes you think in degrees, and degrees are our invention. Let's do it the way a plant would have to do it."

He went to the cupboard and came back with a paper plate, a pin, a ball of string, a ruler, and a jar of dried beans.

"Wrap the string once around the rim of the plate. Cut it where it meets itself."

Sophie did, and held up the piece.

"That is one whole turn," Neil said. "Not 360 of anything. Just once around."

"Okay."

"Fold it in half and mark the fold. That's half a turn. Lay a bean at the rim, then step the folded length around the rim and lay the next one. Keep going."

Sophie laid the fourth bean and stopped. There were two piles: one at the top of the plate, one at the bottom. Every bean after the second had landed on a bean already there.

"Two lanes," she said.

"Fold a fresh piece into thirds."

Three piles this time, evenly spaced, and every new bean joined one of them.

"Three lanes. It's the same failure I got with the protractor."

"Now measure out *x* of the string," Neil said. "0.618 of it. Not a fold — a measurement."

The string was 34 centimeters. Sophie worked it out on her phone, cut a piece 21 centimeters long, and started again: step 21 centimeters around the rim, lay a bean, step again.

Bean four went into a gap. Bean seven went into a smaller gap. Bean twelve went into a gap she hadn't noticed was there. By bean twenty the rim was ringed all the way around, unevenly spaced but with no lanes at all, and no bean sitting on another.

"It never lines up," she said.

"It never gets the chance to," Neil said.
Sophie wrote down what had actually happened, including the parts that went badly. Her string had stretched a little. Her 21-centimeter cut was long by a millimeter or two, and by the last few beans that small error had piled up into a crowded patch on one side of the plate. The three results were still clear enough to keep: half a turn made two lanes, a third of a turn made three, and the measured *x* of a turn made none.

![Three paper plates side by side, each ringed with dried beans: the first showing two crowded piles, the second three, the third an unbroken ring of separated beans.](illustrations/ch04_diagram_three_discs.png)

*book1_ch04_05 — The same experiment at three step sizes: half a turn collects the beans into two lanes, a third of a turn into three, and a step of x of a turn leaves no lanes at all.*

Then Sophie reached into her bag and took out the proportion gauge.

She opened it over the plate and tried to make it say something. She set the two outer tips on a pair of neighboring beans, and the middle tip came down on bare paper between them, which meant nothing at all — there was no third bean there for it to agree or disagree with. She tried the outer tips on beans one and three, then one and five. The middle tip landed in empty space every time.

"It won't read," she said.

"No."

"Why not? It's the same number. You just told me the step is *x*."

"It is the same number," Neil said. "It isn't the same kind of measurement. The gauge tells you one thing: whether a length has been divided into two parts in the ratio *x*. Two ends and a mark in between. That's the only question it can be asked."

Sophie looked at the plate. "And this isn't a length divided in two."

"It's a turn, repeated. There's no mark in between, because there's no in between. Your string measured it. The gauge can't."

Sophie put the gauge back in her bag, then took it out again and set it on the table where she could see it.

"I've been carrying that thing around like it can check anything," she said.

"It can check one thing very well," Neil said.

She wrote in her notebook: *one tool, one kind of question.* Then, underneath: *If I'd only had the gauge, I would have missed this whole chapter.*


"Show me," Sophie said, pulling out a fresh sheet of paper.

*book1_ch04_06 — Three circles compared: 90° and 120° make wasteful spokes; 137.5° fills evenly.*

## The Golden Angle

"Wait," Sophie said, pencil hovering over the paper. "If I just keep marking dots on the same circle, they'll all sit on the edge of the circle. That won't make a spiral—it'll make a ring of dots."

"Excellent catch," Neil said. "You've spotted the key. A circle has a *constant* radius—every point is the same distance from the center. But a flower head doesn't work that way. Think about how a sunflower actually grows. Where do new seeds appear?"

Sophie thought back to the flower head. "In the middle. New seeds form at the center, and the older ones get pushed outward."

"Right. So each new seed starts at the center and ends up a little farther out than the seed before it. To copy that on paper, you have to do two things at once for every dot: turn by the golden angle, *and* step a little farther out from the center. You *grow the radius* as you go."

"So it's not one circle," Sophie said slowly. "It's like... a circle that keeps getting bigger. A spiral."

"Exactly. Turn and step out. Turn and step out. The turning comes from the golden angle. The stepping-out comes from growth. Together they make the spiral."

"But how far out?" Sophie asked. "Each step—is it the same little nudge every time?"

"Look at a real head and measure," Neil said. "The nudges get *smaller* as you go out. Here's the reason, and it's pure bookkeeping of space: every seed wants about the same room. But the room out near the rim of a disk is spread thin—area grows as the *square* of the distance from the center. So to give seed number *n* its fair share of room, it has to sit about the square root of *n* out from the middle." He wrote it small in the corner: **r ≈ c·√n**. "Seed 1 at one step, seed 4 at two steps, seed 9 at three steps. The distance climbs like √n, not like *n*."

"So the outward part isn't phi at all," Sophie said slowly. "The *turn* is phi—137.5° every time. But how far *out* each seed sits is just… sharing the room evenly."

"Two different rules doing two different jobs," Neil said. "Phi handles the turning. Equal room handles the reaching-out. And here's the part worth chewing on: that's how *I* would calculate it, on paper—square-root of *n*. The plant never takes a square root. New seeds are simply born at the center and shove the older ones outward, each keeping about the same elbow room as it goes. Different cause—growth and crowding, not arithmetic—but the *same* pattern comes out. When you grow a phyllotaxis head yourself in the Discovery Zone, you can switch between 'grow from the center' and the √n formula and watch them land in the same place."

Sophie drew a small dot near the center. Then, using the protractor, she measured 137.5 degrees, slid her ruler a few millimeters farther from the center, and marked the second dot.

Another 137.5 degrees, another small step outward. Third dot.

She kept going. Dot after dot, each one turned 137.5 degrees from the previous one and nudged a little farther from the center, winding outward.

After ten dots, she paused and looked at the pattern.

The dots were spreading out beautifully—not clustered together, not leaving obvious gaps. They seemed to fill the page evenly.

"Keep going," Neil encouraged.

Sophie kept marking dots. Twenty dots. Thirty dots.

The pattern became even more striking. The dots formed spirals—just like the sunflower seeds. She could see curved lines connecting them, winding outward from the center.

And still, no dot landed exactly on top of a previous dot.

"It works," Sophie whispered. "The golden angle really does spread them out perfectly."

"Count the spirals," Neil said.

Sophie leaned close and traced the curves with her finger. She counted spirals going one direction, then spirals going the other direction.

"Five spirals one way," she said. "Eight spirals the other way." She looked up. "Five and eight—just like the pineapple."

"Fibonacci numbers," Neil confirmed. "Same pair you counted on the pineapple's scales this morning. Only now you drew them yourself, one dot at a time."

Sophie sat back, amazed.

"So when you place things at the golden angle, you automatically get Fibonacci spirals?"

"You do," Neil said. "The angle *creates* the spirals. That's why the sunflower has 21 and 34 spirals. The flower doesn't 'count' spirals. It just places each seed 137.5 degrees from the previous one, and the spirals emerge automatically."

"That's incredible," Sophie said.

"And here's a bonus that's easy to miss," Neil said, zooming in on the seed head. "There aren't just two families of spirals—clockwise and counterclockwise. Where those two cross, look along the diagonals and you'll find a *third* family. And all three counts are Fibonacci numbers that obey the Fibonacci rule."

Sophie wrote it down:

```
#clockwise + #counterclockwise = #diagonal
e.g.   21 + 34 = 55     (all Fibonacci numbers!)
```

"It's the 'add the last two to get the next' rule again," she said softly. "Even in the spirals themselves."

"Even there," Neil agreed. "Every new seed lands in the biggest gap left by the others—always 137.5 degrees around from the last one. Turn, step out, turn, step out—and whole families of Fibonacci spirals appear, all on their own."

Sophie stared at the crossing arms. "Nobody drew those spirals, though. They're just... there."

"That's the thing to hold on to," Neil said. "The plant never draws a spiral. It only makes one move, over and over. The spirals are what that repeated move *leaves behind*—a signature. Like a fingerprint. You can't see the turning itself, but you can count the arms it left, and the count tells you exactly what the plant was doing." He tapped the seed head. "If you ever want to watch those arm-families sharpen up and swap as a head fills in, there's a bench in the Discovery Zone that grows one seed at a time and highlights whichever pair is winning. But you don't need it to trust the count—you just did it by hand."

"Want to know something even more incredible?" Neil asked.

"Always."

"Let me show you where the golden angle comes from. And I promise it won't come out of nowhere."

He drew a circle in his notebook and pointed to the full turn around it.

"In Chapter 3," he said, "phi split a *length*—a line cut into a longer part and a shorter part. Watch what happens when I bend that same cut around into a circle. Now phi isn't dividing a distance. It's dividing a *lap*—one whole trip around. And a lap is something you can take again, and again, and again."

Sophie nodded slowly. "A repeating version of the same cut."

"That's the whole shift from last chapter to this one. In space, phi told a whorl how much bigger to get each turn—a growth increment. Here, phi tells the plant how far around to step each turn—the same ratio, bent from a straight increment into a *cyclic* one. A lap instead of a length."

"A whole circle is 360 degrees. Now, suppose we want to split that 360 degrees into two portions—a bigger arc and a smaller arc. There are infinitely many ways to do that. But let's split it the golden way: so that the two portions are in the golden ratio. The bigger arc divided by the smaller arc should equal φ."

Sophie nodded. "Like the golden rectangle, but bent around a circle."

"Exactly. Now remember our friend the conjugate—little *x*, equal to 0.618—and its magic rule." Neil wrote:

**1/x = 1 + x**

"Watch what happens if I multiply both sides of that by *x*," he said.

```
1/x = 1 + x
1   = x + x²      (multiply both sides by x)
```

"Beautiful," Neil said, tapping the page. "This tells us something wonderful: a whole of **1** splits naturally into two pieces—an *x* piece and an *x²* piece. And they add up perfectly: x + x² = 1."

Sophie checked it on her calculator. "x is 0.618. x² is 0.618 × 0.618 = 0.382. And 0.618 + 0.382 = 1. It works!"

"So the golden way to split *anything*—a length, or a full circle—is into an *x* portion and an *x²* portion," Neil said. "The big portion is *x* of the whole, the small portion is *x²* of the whole. For our circle, the whole is 360 degrees."

Sophie's eyes lit up. She did the math herself:

```
Big arc   = x  × 360° = 0.618 × 360° = 222.5°
Small arc = x² × 360° = 0.382 × 360° = 137.5°
```

"The small arc is 137.5 degrees!" she said. "The golden angle!"

"And check the ratio of the two arcs," Neil prompted.

Sophie divided. "222.5 ÷ 137.5 = 1.618. That's φ! The two arcs really are in the golden ratio."

"One more thing is hiding in there," Neil said. "Take the small arc away from the big one. How many degrees are left between them?"

Sophie subtracted. "222.5 minus 137.5 is 85 degrees."

"And 85 out of the whole 360?"

She divided, then checked it against her number table from Chapter 3. "0.236... that's *x* cubed — x times x times x."

"It is," Neil said. "Look at the little ladder you just built. The big arc is *x* of the circle, the small arc is *x²*, and the gap between them is *x³*. Each rung is one more multiply by x — the same conjugate, folding the circle finer and finer." He wrote it beside her work:

```
x − x² = x³        →   222.5° − 137.5° = 85° = x³ × 360°
```

Sophie added a note in the margin: *x, x², x³ — the whole angle is just x, taken again and again.*

"There's an even shorter way to write it," Neil added. "Since *x* is just 1 divided by φ, the small portion *x²* is 1 divided by φ². So the golden angle is:"

**360° × x² = 360° ÷ φ² = 137.5°**

Sophie multiplied 1.618 by itself—2.618—and divided 360 by it. "137.5 degrees. The same answer, from φ squared."

She stared at the equations, then at Neil.

"So the golden angle isn't a random number someone picked," she said. "It's just the golden way of cutting a circle in two—the small piece."

"The same phi from the Fibonacci sequence, the same phi from the golden ratio, the same conjugate *x* from our number table," Neil said. "It's all connected. Phi shows up in lengths, in ratios, in areas, in angles—anywhere there's balance and proportion."

Sophie looked down at her circle full of dots.

"So let me keep the two jobs straight," Sophie said. "Phi sets the *turn* — 137.5 degrees, the same for every seed. That's the angular part. The reaching-*out* isn't phi at all; it's the equal-room rule, √n. In Chapter 3 the whorl's radius really did grow by phi — but that was a solid shape swelling in space. Here the head stays flat and just shares the room."

"Good — keep those apart," Neil said. "The turning is phi's one job. The criss-crossing spiral arms you *see* are only what that repeated turn leaves behind. Same phi, doing the single thing it does best on a lap: dividing so that nothing ever lines up twice."

Sophie didn't write that down. She was looking at the protractor.

"Neil — how does a sunflower measure 137.5 degrees?"

"It doesn't."

"Then how does it get the angle right?"

"It doesn't get the angle right either. Nothing in that flower is aiming at 137.5." He tapped the paper plate, still ringed with beans. "Think about what you just did. Did you measure a single angle?"

Sophie thought back. "No. I cut a string and stepped it around the rim."

"How much of a turn was each step?"

"*x* of it. 0.618."

"And what angle is that?"

She hadn't asked. She multiplied: 0.618 × 360.

"222.5 degrees," she said, and stopped. "That's not 137.5."

"Put a protractor on the same step."

Sophie set the protractor down, and then saw it before she had finished lining it up. A protractor doesn't read past 180. Turning 222.5 degrees one way around the circle leaves you in exactly the same place as turning 137.5 degrees the *other* way, and 360 − 222.5 = 137.5.

"It's the same step," she said. "Measured the short way round."

"Say more," Neil said. "What does that tell you about direction?"

Sophie ran her pencil around the circle, once each way. "You can reach the very same seed two ways. Step the *big* arc — x of a lap, 222.5° — going forward. Or step the *small* arc — x² of a lap, 137.5° — going backward. Forward or reverse, clockwise or counter-clockwise — same landing spot." She wrote it down:

```
+222.5°  (x of a lap, forward)   =  −137.5°  (x² of a lap, reverse)
+137.5°  (x² of a lap, forward)  =  −222.5°  (x of a lap, reverse)
```

"So the plant might be turning the long way or the short way," she said. "I can't tell which, just from the finished seeds. Both leave the same arrangement behind."

"Both leave the same fingerprint," Neil agreed. "The circle doesn't care which direction you counted, or whether you stepped by x or by x². It closes on itself. That's what makes a *lap* different from a straight line — forward by the big arc and backward by the small arc are the same move."

"And that's why the plant needs to know none of it," Neil said. "It just advances by a fraction, the ratio, of a turn. We come along afterwards with a protractor, report the shorter of the two arcs between one seed and the next, and get 137.5. The turning is what happens. The angle is what's left over once we've measured it."

Sophie sat with that.

"And 360 isn't the plant's number either," Neil added. "It's ours. But it isn't just made up — it came from the sky."

"The sky?"

"The oldest written record of it is in the Rigveda," Neil said, "a verse by the rishi Dirghatamas. He describes the year as a great wheel: twelve fellies for the months, and *three hundred and sixty spokes* 'which in nowise can be loosened.'" He sketched a wheel and crowded its rim with tick-marks. "They watched the Sun's path, split it into twelve Rashis of about thirty days each, and the whole year came out near three hundred and sixty days. So they built a chakra — a wheel — with three hundred and sixty spokes, and matched the circle to the year. They even subdivided each part into smaller units — kala, vikala — finer angle-measure than anyone else had at the time."

Sophie looked at the crowded wheel. "So 360 comes from counting days."

"From watching the sky, carefully, a very long time ago. It's a good, well-reasoned number — but it's still a *count people chose* to match the year. Suppose they'd chosen 400 instead."

Sophie ran it: *x*² was 0.382, and 0.382 × 400 = 152.8.

"Then the golden angle would be 152.8," she said. "Of the new parts. And the sunflower wouldn't have changed at all."

"Not by a hair. The parts are ours — we counted them off the sky. The fraction is the plant's."

"So what is the plant actually doing?"

"Putting each new bud in the roomiest space the ones before it left behind," Neil said. "Do that over and over at the growing tip and you get the advance you just made with the string. That's the rule. 137.5 is the report."

"Wait, so" — Sophie's eyes narrowed — "if every bud just grabs the emptiest spot going, how does that end up being the *one* angle that packs the most seeds? That feels like luck."

"It isn't luck, and here's the lovely part," Neil said. "Follow 'roomiest gap, every time' for lap after lap, and there's only one turn it can settle into — the golden one. Any turn that's a simple fraction eventually lines seeds up in spokes and leaves big gaps, so the 'roomiest gap' rule keeps steering away from it. The one turn it never has to steer away from is the golden angle. The rule *converges* to it, all on its own."

"So the plant isn't solving a puzzle," Sophie said slowly. "It's just... following the rule, and the rule only has one place to land."

"That's it. It's almost as if nature already knew the answer and simply carried out the plan to reach it — solving the whole problem at once, then executing it one seed at a time. The plant fits the most seeds it possibly can into the space it has, without ever measuring a thing. The best solution and the simple rule turn out to be the same move."

Sophie looked back at the sunflower photographs on the table, at her own pencil numbers running out from the center.

"My readings weren't 137.5," she said. "They were about 137. One of them was 139, I think — I didn't write it down."

"Write it down next time," Neil said. "They scatter. They scatter on every head anyone has ever measured, and on some heads badly, because a sunflower grows in wind and heat and drought and doesn't do arithmetic. If the plant were reading a protractor you'd expect the numbers to come out sharp. They don't. That's not a problem with the flowers. It's a hint about what's really going on."

"But 137.5 is in every book about this," Sophie said.

"It's a good number to know," Neil said. "It just isn't a number anything is aiming at."

He went back to sorting the photographs.

Sophie turned to a fresh page and wrote it in her own words, because she wanted to be sure she still had it tomorrow:

*The plant turns. The angle is only what my protractor makes of the turning.*

## The Function

"Can I ask a question?" Sophie said.

They'd moved outside to Neil's garden, where he had several potted succulents with beautiful spiral rosettes.

"Of course," Neil said.

"The golden angle spreads things out evenly. I get that. But *why* does the sunflower do it? Why not just place seeds randomly?"

Neil smiled, then held up a hand, gently. "Good question — and let's stay careful with that word, *why*. It's an easy place to get ahead of ourselves, because we can't climb inside the flower and read its reasons. What we *can* do is watch what the pattern actually gives the plant, then offer a theory — and hold it loosely until more patterns test it. So let's do that. Here's a *theory*, not a verdict."

"Okay. What's the theory?"

"Start with something we can at least suppose — that a sunflower does better when it makes more seeds *and* catches more light. *If* that's so, the golden turn earns its keep in *three* ways at once — and the first one begins long before there's ever a flower."

"Before the flower?"

"Think about the plant growing up. Leaf by leaf, all the way up the stalk. Now — where does each new leaf go?"

Sophie glanced at the spiral rosettes in front of her. "At the golden angle? From the one below it?"

"At the golden angle from the one below it," Neil said. "So no leaf sits directly over another. Every leaf is turned a little farther round the stem, and each one catches sunlight without throwing the leaves beneath it into shadow. That's the *first* benefit — and it's happening the whole time the plant is climbing toward its flower."

"So the leaves are already using the turn," Sophie said. "Before there's a single seed."

"Before there's a single seed. Now — the flower head. That's where the *second* benefit shows up." He pulled out his phone and showed her a simulation. On the screen, a circle filled with dots. The first simulation placed dots at random angles.

"Random placement," Neil said.

The dots created clusters in some areas and gaps in others. It looked messy and inefficient.

"Now watch the golden angle," Neil said.

He restarted the simulation. This time, each dot was placed at 137.5 degrees from the previous one.

The dots spread out evenly, filling the circle with almost no gaps. They packed together tightly but without overlapping.

"Same number of dots," Neil said. "But the golden angle version uses the space better. More dots fit in the same area."

"So the flower can make more seeds," Sophie said.

"That's what we can *see and measure*," Neil said. "The golden turn packs more into the same plate than a random turn does. That part isn't a guess — you can count it. Whether the plant 'wants' more seeds is the part we're only supposing."

"And there's a *third* benefit — this one about staying upright," Neil added. "The seed head is a heavy, flat plate of seeds balanced on a single stalk — like a plate balanced on one finger. If the seeds piled up on one side, it would tip over and snap the stalk."

"Like a see-saw with everyone on one end," Sophie said.

"Exactly. So the flower has a *balancing* problem as well as a *packing* one. The angle that drops every new seed into the biggest empty gap *also* keeps the weight spread evenly across the whole plate. And a balanced head isn't only about not tipping in the wind — a head that's evenly loaded is one the plant can *turn*. Young sunflowers swing to face the sun as it crosses the sky, tracking it from east to west to soak up as much light as they can. That's far easier with a balanced plate than a lopsided one."

"So it packs the seeds, keeps the head balanced, *and* the balance helps it follow the sun," Sophie said.

"That's the idea. And here's a small mystery to tuck away," Neil said. "Remember the whole turn split into x and x² — the big reach and the small draw-in? There's a *third* piece hiding one level down: x³, the little leftover we found in the arcs. x and x² we can point to — the spread, the squeeze. What x³ might be *doing* for the plant, if anything, we honestly don't know. It's more mysterious — a thread we're not ready to pull yet. Keep it in the corner of your eye."

Sophie's eyes lit up. "So the golden angle does *three* jobs at once. Light for the leaves, tight packing for the seeds, and a balanced head the plant can turn to the sun."

"That's a theory worth keeping," Neil said. "Three benefits we can actually watch: light up the stem, packing in the plate, and balance that lets the head track the sun. Hold onto it — but hold it *loosely*. When we look at pine cones and pineapples and shells, we may find the very same turn doing something the sunflower never needed. If we do, we don't blame the plant — we improve the theory."

Sophie chewed her pencil. "Is there maybe *one* reason underneath all of them?"

Neil was quiet for a moment. "Here's a thought to carry, not to finish today. You already found that one lap of the turn splits into two arcs — a big reach, x of the lap, and a small draw-in, x² of the lap. A spread and a squeeze, folded into every single turn. And each turn lays down exactly *one* seed. It's as if the plant runs a little rhythm — spread out, draw in — and every cycle *freezes* one seed out of the motion, like a single frame caught from something that never stops moving."

"One seed per cycle," Sophie said slowly.

"One seed per cycle. If that turns out to be the real story, then packing and balance aren't the deepest reason at all — they're the tidy surface left behind by that spread-and-squeeze underneath. We're a couple of books away from doing it properly. But keep the picture: an expansion by x, a compression by x², over and over, and a seed dropped each time the wheel comes round."

Sophie wrote in the margin: *pack + balance = a good theory. one seed per cycle = the question underneath it.*

"One more honest thing," Neil said. "Mathematicians have written down a formula that *places* the seeds — give it the golden angle and it draws a sunflower you can barely tell from a real one. But the ones who built it will tell you straight: it *describes* the pattern, it doesn't *explain* why the plant lands on exactly this turn and no other. Nobody has shown that a plant's genes could aim for 137.5 degrees that precisely — the accuracy is finer than genes are known to work. So the formula draws the flower beautifully and still leaves the *why* wide open."

"So even the experts have a hole in it," Sophie said.

"A real one," Neil said. "Which is exactly why we hold the theory loosely. A description that fits isn't the same as a reason that's proven. Remember that whenever a neat formula tempts you to stop asking."

Sophie picked up one of the succulent plants and looked at it from above.

The leaves formed a perfect spiral rosette, each leaf positioned at the golden angle from the previous one.

"And this plant?" she asked.

"Same principle," Neil said. "In fact, it's that *first* benefit again — the very thing the sunflower's own leaves do up its stem. Here it's just laid out on a flatter, more planar surface — a rosette instead of a tall stalk — so you can take in the whole spiral at a glance. It's about light, not seeds. Each leaf needs sunlight to photosynthesize. If the leaves were stacked directly on top of each other, the lower ones would be in shadow."

"But with the golden angle," Sophie said, tracing the spiral with her finger, "each leaf is rotated just enough that it gets some sunlight. They're staggered."

"Right. Maximum light capture, minimum shadow. It's the optimal arrangement."

Sophie set the succulent down carefully.

"So it's not just pretty," she said quietly. "It's survival. Plants that use the golden angle make more seeds, catch more light, and survive better than plants that don't."

"Nature is beautiful," Neil said. "But nature is also ruthlessly efficient. The beauty and the efficiency are the same thing."

## Everywhere

Over the next hour, Sophie and Neil found the same angle everywhere they looked. The pine cone and pineapple from the farm were already on the table—8 and 13 on the cone, families of 5, 8, and 13 on the pineapple. Now Neil added photographs: daisies, black-eyed Susans, an artichoke, the great disk of a sunflower. Every one of them arranged its petals or seeds or scales with the same golden turn.

"It's universal," Sophie said, looking at the collection spread across Neil's table. "Different plants, different parts of the world, different purposes—but the same angle."

"137.5 degrees," Neil agreed. "The angle that divides a circle perfectly. The angle that emerges from phi. The same signature, over and over, on things that never met."

"A signature," Sophie repeated. "Like the spiral arms. Something the plant didn't set out to draw, but left behind anyway."

"That's exactly the word," Neil said. "Think of it as nature's fingerprint. When you find the same fingerprint on a pine cone, a pineapple, and a daisy, you stop asking whether it's a coincidence. You start asking what all three are *doing* that leaves the same mark. We don't have the whole answer yet. But the fingerprint tells us there's one thing to find, not three."

Sophie leaned over the sunflower photograph and started counting the spiral arms near the rim. "Twenty-one one way," she said, "thirty-four the other." Then she moved her finger to the smaller spirals nearer the center and counted again. "But in here it's only thirteen and twenty-one. It's the *same head*. How can it have two different pairs of numbers?"

"A mathematician named Turing noticed the very same thing," Neil said. "Count the outer florets and you get one Fibonacci pair; count the inner ones and you get the pair before it. Nothing changed but where you looked. And here's the part he loved: moving your eye from the outside in is like watching the head *grow*, only backward. Every seed near the rim was once a seed near the center. So the numbers you read across the flower in space are the same numbers the flower climbed through in time."

"Space or time," Sophie said slowly, "the same ladder of numbers."

"Hold onto that," Neil said. "You'll need it before the day's out."

Sophie pulled out her field journal and started sketching. She drew a circle and marked dots at the golden angle. She drew the spirals that emerged. She wrote the equation: 360° ÷ φ² = 137.5°.

"We've seen patterns in space," she said, thinking aloud as she drew. "Spirals that grow outward in repeating cycles. Angles that divide circles. Both governed by phi."

"Right," Neil said.

"What about patterns in time?" Sophie asked, looking up. "Rhythms? Beats? Do those follow phi too?"

Neil's smile widened. Instead of answering, he pulled up a spinning disk on his screen—a seed-head that turned a little further every frame. "Watch the edge," he said. As it spun, the whole pattern seemed to lock and hold still, as if someone had frozen it, even though it never stopped turning. Then it slowly crawled backward.

"It's not really frozen," Sophie said, leaning in. "It's still spinning. It just *looks* stopped."

"Your eye takes a snapshot a fixed number of times a second," Neil said. "When the turn between snapshots lines up with the pattern's own step, the motion hides. Same golden number—only now it's a signature written in *time* instead of space. There's a bench for that too, if you want to chase it later. But that," he tapped the screen, "is the door to tomorrow."

"Which is?"

"The ocean. Pack a bag."

"The ocean?" Sophie asked.

"Waves," Neil said simply. "Patterns that move through time and space instead of just space. Same mathematics, different medium."

## Explore It Yourself

*Try these at home or at school. Some use kitchen-table objects; others use the free tools on the INW book series website (Imagine Nature's Wheelwork). This chapter's patterns are harder to pin down by hand than last chapter's — a turning, repeating thing is difficult to freeze and measure with a protractor — so a few of these lean on the Discovery Zone benches, where you can grow a seed head one seed at a time and even stop its spin to look.*

#### Activity 1: Sight Down a Leafy Stalk

**What You Need:** A leafy plant stalk (mint, basil, a garden weed, or a houseplant); a protractor; a pencil

**Instructions:**
1. Find the youngest leaf at the top and the one born just before it, a little lower down.
2. Sight straight down the stalk from above, or look along it, so the leaves stack around the stem.
3. Line your protractor up on the stem and measure the turn from one leaf to the next one down.
4. Step down the stalk, leaf by leaf, checking the turn each time.

**Questions:**
- How big was the turn from each leaf to the one just below it?
- Was it about the same all the way down the stalk?
- The leaves are easy to put in birth order — youngest on top, oldest at the bottom. Why does that make the stalk the easiest place to *catch* the golden angle with a protractor?

**Key Insight:** On a stalk, births are spread out in a line you can read, so the turn between them — about 137.5° — is something you can actually measure. This is the first rung of the ladder: a pattern you can *measure*.

#### Activity 2: Count the Spirals on a Pineapple and a Pine Cone

**What You Need:** A pineapple and a pine cone (a sunflower head works too)

**Instructions:**
1. Stand the pineapple upright (stem end down) and hold it straight while you count — it's much easier than tilting it around.
2. Look closely at one scale: it's a diamond (trapezoid) shape, and its two slanting *edges* each point along a spiral family. Pick one slant and count how many arms of that slant you cross going *once all the way around* the pineapple — that's your first count. Then follow the other slanting edge around for your second count. (You'll often get 8 arms one way and 13 the other.)
3. For the third family (often 5), don't follow an edge — follow the nearly-flat diagonal that runs corner-to-corner across the diamonds, and count those arms around.
4. For the pine cone, stand it upright and count at the *bottom* end, where the scales fan out widest: trace the rows curving one way, then the rows curving the other way.
5. Write down each pair of counts.

**Questions:**
- On the pine cone, did you get 8 one way and 13 the other?
- On the pineapple, did you find families of 5, 8, and 13?
- Are your counts always neighbors in the Fibonacci sequence (5, 8, 13, 21…)?

**Key Insight:** You can't easily rank a pine cone's scales by birth order anymore — but you *can* still count the spiral families, and they're always Fibonacci neighbors. This is the second rung: a pattern you can no longer measure one step at a time, but can still *count*.

#### Activity 3: Plant Seeds at the Golden Angle

**What You Need:** Paper; a protractor; a pencil; small stickers or dots

**Instructions:**
1. Mark a center dot on your paper.
2. Place a seed-dot, then turn 137.5° and place the next, and the next — always turning the same golden angle.
3. After 20–30 dots, look at the overall pattern.
4. Now try again with a "wrong" angle like 90° or 120°.

**Questions:**
- What appeared on its own when you used 137.5°, and did any dots crowd each other?
- What happened with the "wrong" angles — did the dots form wasteful spokes with big gaps?
- Count the spiral arms your dots made — were they Fibonacci neighbors, just like the pineapple?

#### Activity 4: The Bean Plate (No Protractor Allowed)

**What You Need:** A paper plate; string; scissors; a ruler; a jar of dried beans (or buttons, or coins)

**Instructions:**
1. Wrap the string once around the rim of the plate and cut it where it meets itself. That length is one whole turn.
2. Fold it in half. Lay a bean on the rim, then step the folded length around the rim and lay the next bean. Keep going to about twenty beans, or until you are sure what is happening.
3. Start over with a fresh string folded into thirds.
4. Start over again, but this time *measure* x of the string with the ruler — 0.618 of its length — instead of folding it. Step that length around the rim.
5. Write down all three results, including anything that went wrong: a stretched string, a cut that was slightly long, beans crowding on one side by the end.

**Questions:**
- How many lanes did half a turn make? A third of a turn?
