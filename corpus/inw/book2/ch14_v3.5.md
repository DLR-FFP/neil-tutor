## Explore It Yourself

*Try these at home or at school. Some use things you can find around you; others use the free tools on the INW book series website.*

### Activity 14.1 — The Doubling Cubes

**What you need:** 8 identical sugar cubes, dice, or building blocks.

**Predict first:** Before building, write down how many *new* cubes you think appear when a 1×1×1 cube doubles to 2×2×2.

Start with one cube — the original. Build a 2×2×2 block around it. Count only the *new* ones: **7**. Now try the same in one dimension (a line of 1 doubles to 2 — gained 1) and two dimensions (a 1×1 square doubles to 2×2 — gained 3).

Record:

| Dimensions | Formula | Increment |
|-----------|---------|-----------|
| 1D        | 2¹−1   |           |
| 2D        | 2²−1   |           |
| 3D        | 2³−1   |           |

Was your prediction right? Why does the increment grow so fast with dimension?

### Activity 14.2 — Concentric Circle Areas

**What you need:** compass or two round objects (one with double the radius of the other), ruler, pencil, paper.

**Predict first:** If a circle doubles its radius, how many times bigger is its area? How much *new* area is added?

Draw a small circle with radius r (say 2 cm). Measure and calculate its area (πr²). Now draw a concentric circle with radius 2r (4 cm). Calculate *its* area. Subtract to find the new ring area. Divide the ring area by the original circle area.

| | Radius | Area | New area | New ÷ Original |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inner circle | r = 2 cm | | — | — |
| Outer circle | 2r = 4 cm | | | |

You should find the ring holds **3** times the original (because 2² − 1 = 3 in 2D). For 3D spheres the factor is **7** (because 2³ − 1 = 7) — but you can't easily draw that! Explain in one sentence *why* adding a dimension multiplies the increment.
