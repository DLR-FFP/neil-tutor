The Golden Section Gauge (Caliper)
A Golden Section Gauge automatically divides any span into the golden ratio φ ≈ 1.618. The device has four physical arm segments pinned together. Points B and C sit on the two long arms (AF and AH respectively); AB and AC are not separate pieces — they are just the upper portions of the long arms, marked as virtual reference lengths.
Parts list (340 mm design)
Arm 1: AF = 340 mm — left long arm, pivot at A (top) and F (bottom tip). Pivot B is a marked point on this arm, 130 mm from A.
Arm 2: AH = 340 mm — right long arm, pivot at A (top) and H (bottom tip). Pivot C is a marked point on this arm, 130 mm from A.
Arm 3: BE–EG = 210 mm total — inner cross arm from B through pivot E to tip G. BE = 130 mm, EG = 80 mm.
Arm 4: CE–EG — inner cross arm from C through the same pivot E. CE = 130 mm.
Pivots: A (shared top of both long arms), B (on arm AF), C (on arm AH), E (crossing point of inner arms), G (lower tip, between B and C tips).
AF / BG = 340 / 210 ≈ 1.619 ≈ φ
AB / BE = 130 / 130 — same length
BE / EG = 130 / 80 ≈ 1.625 ≈ φ
Step-by-step construction
Cut two long arms 340 mm each (6 mm × 20 mm hardwood strip). Mark point A at one end. Mark pivot point B (arm 1) and C (arm 2) at 130 mm from A. Drill pivot holes at A, B (arm 1), C (arm 2), and at the tip ends F and H.
Cut two inner cross arms: both 210 mm. Mark E at 130 mm from one end (giving BE = CE = 130 mm, EG = 80 mm). Drill pivot holes at B-end, E (crossing point), and G-tip.
Assemble: bolt A (top, shared). Bolt B through arm 1 and inner arm 3. Bolt C through arm 2 and inner arm 4. Bolt E where the two inner arms cross. G is the free lower tip.
Open the caliper — F and H spread apart; BG and AH stay in the golden ratio at all opening widths.

The Phi Scissor Caliper
The Phi Scissor Caliper needs only two identical pointed bars and one pivot. Wherever the short tips pinch together, the long tips automatically span exactly φ ≈ 1.618 times wider — at every opening angle — because both tip pairs swing through the same angle at pivot P, keeping the two triangles similar and the gap ratio equal to long ÷ short.
Parts list (34 cm ★ recommended)
2 identical bars, each 34 cm total — mark and punch one hole at P, exactly 13 cm from one pointed end (21 cm from the other). Choose: poster-board strip pointed at both ends, wooden kabob skewer (already pointed), or drinking straw (snip ends to points).
1 pivot at P — choose: brass paper fastener (split pin), push pin + eraser cap, or rubber band tied figure-8 through both holes.
Tools — ruler, pencil, push pin for punching holes.
Fibonacci size options (short + long = bar): 3+5=8 cm · 5+8=13 cm · 8+13=21 cm · 13+21=34 cm ★ · 21+34=55 cm. Higher pairs give a ratio closer to true φ = 1.6180…
bar = short + long = 13 + 21 = 34 cm (Fibonacci ★)
big gap ÷ small gap = long ÷ short = 21 ÷ 13 ≈ 1.615 ≈ φ
ratio holds at every opening angle
Step-by-step construction
Cut or select two identical bars, each 34 cm. Both ends must come to a point (skewers and straws are already pointed).
Stack the two bars perfectly on top of each other, both ends flush. Measure 13 cm from one end and mark point P. Double-check: P must be 21 cm from the other end.
Hold both bars together and push a push pin straight through both at P simultaneously — this guarantees the holes align. Wiggle the pin to widen the hole until your pivot fastener passes through smoothly.
Fit the pivot: thread the brass split pin (or rubber band in a figure-8) through both holes and secure. The bars should swing freely around P.
Calibrate: squeeze the short tips until the small gap is 5 cm → confirm the long tips span ≈ 8 cm (8 ÷ 5 = 1.6 ≈ φ ✓). Closed check: both short tips meet and both long tips meet.

About this lab
Parastichies are the criss-crossing spiral arms on sunflower heads, pinecones and pineapples. Each seed sits at polar coordinates r = c·√n, θ = n·α. Only the golden angle (137.508°, turn fraction x² = 1/φ² ≈ 0.38197) makes every visible arm-number pair a consecutive Fibonacci pair.
Rational angles collapse the head into spokes; irrational-but-near-rational angles give spokes with a slow twist. Sweep α and watch order emerge and dissolve.
The Cylinder surface switches to the cylindrical model (φ = n·α, r = const, H = n·h) of Fowler, Hanan & Prusinkiewicz (Modelling Spiral Phyllotaxis, 1989) — the same divergence mathematics wrapped around a stalk, as in pine cones and pineapples. Vertical step h and tilt become visible controls.
User guide

Pine cone / Pineapple chips (cylinder mode) — jump to the measured divergence angles 137.5° and 138.1395°.
Teacher guide

About this lab
A zoetrope turns a sequence of stills into motion by showing them one at a time. Here the browser's own frame rate (~60 fps) is the stroboscope: each frame the disk is rotated by a fixed angle. When that angle matches a harmonic of the disk's symmetry, successive frames line up and the disk seems to freeze, crawl, or run backwards.
This is observational sampling of a dynamic process — the same aliasing that makes wagon wheels spin backward on film. Spin the golden spiral at 137.508°/frame and each seed lands where the previous seed was: the head appears to hold still while new seeds bloom outward, like John Edmark's Blooms.

Phyllotaxis User Guide
A friendly STEM guide for ages 13–18: experiment, notice patterns, ask why they happen, and test ideas like a mathematician and scientist.
Close
1) What are you looking at?
This simulator places each new “seed” a little farther from the center and turns it by the same fraction of a full circle each time. That simple rule can create spoke-like patterns, spiral families, and remarkably even packing—just like the seed heads you see in sunflowers and daisies.
2) Fast start
Choose the Golden preset.
Set Seed count above 1000.
Switch between Center-out growth and Static final arrangement.
Turn on Parastichy overlays in static mode.
Watch the phase plot, continued fraction, and nearest rational boxes as you change the turn fraction.
3) Formula entry and presets
Type exact fractions like 2/7 or 5/13.
Type formulas like (sqrt(5)-1)/2 or 1/pi.
Use the preset library to jump to famous values quickly and compare how they spread seeds.
4) Sweep range, rotary speed, and scrubbing
Set a minimum and maximum turn fraction for the sweep window. Decimal endpoints like 0.6 and 0.667 are accepted, and accidental repeated dots are cleaned up safely.
Use the speed level control from 1 to 9. Level 1 is slowest; level 9 is fastest.
The dial maps to reliable sweep increments from 1e-7 up to 1e-4 turns per frame.
Use the scrub slider to stop exactly where a pattern becomes interesting.
5) What to investigate
Rational turns like 1/3 or 2/5 create obvious spokes because the angle repeats after only a few steps.
Irrational turns avoid repeating exactly, which spreads seeds around the circle more evenly.
The golden-related turns are special because they are unusually hard to imitate with simple fractions.
6) Try these mini challenges
Can you find a turn fraction that gives exactly 7 strong spokes?
Can you use the scrubber to stop at a pattern that looks almost radial but is not exactly repeating?
Which preset gives the most even-looking fill at 2000 seeds?
How does changing the denominator cap affect the parastichy overlay you see?
7) Reading the diagnostics
Nearest rational: the simple fraction closest to your current turn.
Parastichy pair: the two step sizes used to draw visible spiral families.
Continued fraction: a compact code that reveals the best nested rational approximations.
CF convergents: the sequence of fractions that get closer and closer to your current turn.
8) Suggested STEM discussion prompts
Why would a plant benefit from not stacking too many seeds along the same ray?
How does a local rule produce a large-scale pattern?
What is the difference between “looks random” and “is evenly distributed”?
Why are some irrational numbers better at avoiding repetition than others?

About Nature's Calc
Close
Nature's Calc is an integrated set of visual explorations of the mathematics found in nature's patterns — from the golden ratio φ to Pascal's triangle, Pingala's ancient meter counting, and the 3D Maha Meru. The collection is built in the spirit of Imagine Nature's Wheelwork (INW): making nature's patterns clear, visual, and usable for students, teachers, and curious learners.
The unifying thread
Every lab in this collection is connected by a single principle: simple rules generate infinite, beautiful structure. The golden ratio x = 1/φ ≈ 0.618 satisfies 1/x = 1 + x — so dividing a unit into x and x² preserves the whole. Pingala's enumeration of long and short syllables builds the same triangle that Blaise Pascal made famous in Europe. The spiral in a sunflower and the Fibonacci sequence both emerge from the same counting. These labs make those connections visible and interactive.
Phi labs — the golden conjugate x
All phi-related labs express lengths as powers of x = 1/φ ≈ 0.618, never as φ directly. φ appears only through the identity 1/x = 1 + x = φ. This choice keeps the fractions honest: x + x² = 1, xⁿ = xⁿ⁺¹ + xⁿ⁺².
Pascal / Meru-prastāra
Pingala's Chandaḥśāstra (c. 300 BCE) enumerated poetic meter patterns using L (laghu, short) and G (guru, long) syllables. Halāyudha's 10th-century commentary arranged them as meru-prastāra, a staircase of numbers equivalent to what Europe later called Pascal's triangle. This collection foregrounds the older Indian story while showing all of the same combinatorial structure.

Each lab opens in its own full-controls view. Use ← Home (top-right button) to return to the landing page and choose another lab. Inside each lab, the sidebar shows that lab's own About, User Guide, and Teacher Guide buttons.
Phi stream (Labs 1–6)
Phi Division: Linear or circular views. Depth slider controls how many x-power levels are shown. Animate reveals segments one at a time.
Phi Spiral: Three construction types — square (golden rectangle), golden triangle, and equilateral Padovan. Toggle guide edges and the true logarithmic spiral overlay.
Phi Derivation: Three perspectives — quantum change, linear balance, and circular decagon convergence. Step through the derivation.
Phi Construction: Five step-by-step ruler-and-compass constructions. Use the Step slider at the bottom of the canvas to advance through each construction.
DIY Caliper & Ruler: Instructions for building physical golden-ratio measurement tools.
Phyllotaxis Spiral: Adjust the angle fraction and see how packing changes. Compare irrational and rational turns.
Number pattern stream (Labs 7–10)
Pascal: Choose display mode, highlight pattern, and click cells. Animate builds row by row.
Pingala: Set syllable length, choose encoding (L/G or S/L), click pattern cards. Animate cycles through them.
Mod-n: Drag the n slider. All-residues mode shows the full color landscape; Focus mode highlights one residue class.
3D Maha Meru: Layer slice → Stacked → Tetrahedron. Drag the canvas to rotate in 3D modes.
General controls (inside any lab)
Animate — triggers a context-sensitive step-through animation for the active lab.
Save PNG — exports the current canvas view.
Reset — restores all controls to their defaults for the current lab.
Teacher Guide — Nature's Calc Collection
Close
Use this collection as an integrated unit on pattern, structure, and number. The strongest strategy is to return to a small set of ideas — ratio, self-similarity, recursion, enumeration, symmetry — while changing the representation from lab to lab. Students who predict before running and revise after observing learn more than those who simply adjust sliders.
Suggested progression
Phi Division Lab — introduce the golden ratio through a concrete partition: one unit splits into x ≈ 0.618 and x² ≈ 0.382, with x + x² = 1. Establish the language of x-powers before anything else.
Phi Spiral Lab — show how the same ratio generates a spiral from repeated geometric subdivision. Compare the constructed spiral to the true logarithmic spiral overlay.
Phi Derivation Lab — move from geometry to algebra: where does x come from? The three perspectives (quantum, linear, circular) each offer a different route to the same identity 1/x = 1 + x.
Phi Construction Lab — step through ruler-and-compass constructions. Students can replicate these physically. Ask them to identify x-lengths in real objects using the DIY Caliper instructions.
Phyllotaxis Spiral — connect φ to biology. Why does the golden angle pack seeds optimally? What happens with rational turns?
Pascal Pattern Lab — shift from ratio to counting. Introduce Pascal's triangle through the Fibonacci slashes, then explore parity, primes, and row sums.
Pingala Meter Lab — show that the same triangle was derived independently in ancient India through poetic meter enumeration. This provides historical breadth and a non-European framing.
Mod-n Lab — explore number theory through color. n = 2 gives the familiar Sierpiński pattern; larger n reveals new structures.
3D Maha Meru — extend the 2D triangle to three dimensions. Introduce trinomial coefficients as a natural generalization.
Instructional goals
Help students move from describing structure to explaining it.
Encourage prediction before running and evidence-based revision afterward.
Use multiple representations: geometric, numeric, algebraic, verbal.
Ask students to find the same pattern across labs (e.g. Fibonacci numbers appear in both the Phi Division and the Pascal labs).
Where possible, connect to physical measurement — the DIY Caliper lab supports this directly.
